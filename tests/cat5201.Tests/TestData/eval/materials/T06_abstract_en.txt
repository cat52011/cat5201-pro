Retrieval-Augmented Generation and the Limits of Citation

Large language models produce fluent text that is not always true. Retrieval-augmented
generation (RAG) attempts to constrain the model by supplying passages drawn from an
external corpus at inference time, so that each assertion can be traced back to a
document rather than to the model's parameters. We evaluated this claim on a corpus of
1,248 question-answer pairs spanning finance, public health and municipal regulation,
scoring each answer for factual support against the retrieved passages.

Retrieval reduced unsupported assertions by 34% relative to the unaugmented baseline,
but did not eliminate them. Two failure modes persisted. First, the model cited a
retrieved passage that was topically related yet did not contain the asserted figure;
human raters accepted such answers more readily than unsourced ones, which suggests that
the presence of a citation shifts reader trust independently of whether the citation
supports the claim. Second, when retrieval returned nothing relevant, the model
occasionally synthesised a plausible passage rather than declining to answer.

The cost of retrieval was not negligible: end-to-end latency increased by a factor of
1.8, and the pipeline required a maintained index whose staleness became its own source
of error. We conclude that retrieval improves traceability but does not by itself
establish truth, and that evaluation protocols which count citations rather than verify
them will overstate the improvement. These limitations apply with particular force to
questions whose answers change over time, for which no static index is adequate.
